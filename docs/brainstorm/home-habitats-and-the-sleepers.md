# Home habitats, the sleepers, and the movement to resettle Prospect

**Not canon. Brainstorm only** (see `README.md`). The owner explicitly said none of this is close to canon (2026-10-19): it's for after the playtest and after the game's core mechanics exist. Where it touches existing canon, the bible section is cited so a later session knows what would have to change.

## Session 1 (2026-10-19)

### Where it started

The owner wants two things for the finished game:

1. **Home habitats, like Achaea's cities.** A player eventually picks a habitat they call home. The current shape (Elsewhere as the newbie zone, other habitats as level-gated areas) would be replaced: every habitat, and Prospect itself, should be worth playing in at every level, so a small world feels bigger.
2. **An overall story goal.** A movement spread across the habitats wants to resettle Prospect, learning from Haven's failure. It is the reason player characters are being woken from cryosleep.

### Relevant existing canon

- Seven major habitats are Established, one bible section each (§§11.2–11.4, 11.8–11.11). §11.6 (owner, 2026-10-18): the habitats are insular and self-governing, with no Constellation-wide law, body or agreement. A *movement* spread across habitats doesn't conflict with that; a jointly run *institution* would.
- §11.5: the habitats chose not to rebuild Haven because of the cost, the biological hazard, and the risk of restoring a former political centre. How the decision was made is open.
- §11.2.4 already says Elsewhere must stay meaningful to advanced players, not be a tutorial-only area. Level-agnostic habitats extend that direction rather than reverse it.
- §13.2: citizenship rules are Unresolved, which leaves room for home-habitat choice.
- The playtest spec treats cryosleep as a starting convention with no universal explanation. The owner considers the current cast and locations playtest scaffolding, not the base of the eventual game, so personas and the spec are not constraints here.

### Owner's leanings so far

**Level-agnostic habitats.** Lore's suggestion, not yet discussed further: habitats are civilised, social and political space. Danger sits in each habitat's frontier (Freehold's derelict hulls, Tailings' platforms, Lantern's specimen work, Anchorage's archive trips into Haven), and in the surface and Haven's ruins, which everyone reaches from their own home. Difficulty comes from depth or distance, not from which habitat you're in (consistent with §13.4: older isn't automatically harder).

**Three joinable home habitats.** The others are neutral areas, still visitable. The owner hasn't chosen which three yet, and may rework some habitats.

**Chargen gets a starting-habitat choice**, like species. Players don't need to know why they were woken, and starting habitat has no gameplay effect. The home habitat is chosen later in play and affects gameplay from then on.

**Sleepers have no memory of their life before.** That avoids the player not knowing what their character remembers. The movement wakes these sleepers *because* they have no memory and no ties to the Constellation, so they're more likely to join.

**Why they have no memory is never resolved.** It's an in-world mystery. The official account is a side effect of very long storage. NPCs who know about the movement have conspiracy theories, some genuinely compelling (for instance, that the movement causes the memory loss). The game never proves any of them, and players choose which story to believe.

**The movement's history:**

- It began as a fringe group that never let go of the idea of rebuilding Haven and resented the Constellation for abandoning it. (Lore's suggestion from the options below: its roots could go back to the people who lost the post-Bloom argument against rebuilding, §11.5.)
- About 50 years ago it grew sharply. It now spans a spectrum: patient idealists who want to win the argument slowly until it's the majority view, and zealots who believe the political route will never work.
- The zealots grew. Some of their actions backfired; some were unethical from the start. The Constellation now remembers the movement for its zealots, and no longer sees it as peaceful.
- Implied chain: failures → public hostility → nobody in the Constellation will join → wake people who don't know the movement's history.

**The plan is not one city.** The movement's reading of Haven: a single huge city was inherently unstable. Its plan is to bring the Constellation's variety back down to the surface, as several settlements with different ideologies coexisting, trading and arguing, rather than one Haven again. (This also echoes the original nine settlements, §8.1.)

**The goal stays aspirational.** Even if it ever happened, it would take decades. Gameplay is preparatory work that helps the project or gets it ready, not building it.

**The movement stays hidden from new players.** Nobody greets a fresh sleeper with "we woke you, here's why."

**Cryosleep is ordinary in the Constellation.** People use it for medical reasons, for personal reasons ("wake me in fifty years, when things are better"), and for incarceration. The old Bloom-era frozen have been kept alive out of moral duty, since letting them die would be wrong. But nearly everyone woken in the last 50–100 years had slept a couple of decades at most. The movement slips awakenings of old evacuees in among routine revivals.

### Lore's notes on the cover story (for the owner to weigh)

- **It works, and amnesia hides the anachronism.** A sleeper with no memory can't notice they've lost 150 years.
- **The records have to lie.** An old evacuee would have to be logged as a recent sleeper, so the movement needs people inside revival clinics or records offices, and **each player's own paperwork is false**. That's a possible personal hook.
- **Anchorage holds Haven's records** (§11.8). A player looking for who they were might find that Anchorage's evacuee rolls don't match their file.
- **Amnesia needs a plausible base rate.** If memory loss only happens after very long sleeps, a "twenty-year sleeper" with total amnesia is suspicious. Either occasional memory loss after short sleeps is a known risk (so it's cover), or the *rising number* of blank sleepers is exactly the evidence the conspiracy theorists cite. Both could be true.
- **Revival sites in several habitats** (needed for the starting-habitat choice) have to be permitted locally (§11.6). With the movement hidden inside routine revivals, that's simply ordinary clinics in each habitat.

### Open: spoilers in a persistent multiplayer game

The owner's concern: nothing stops a veteran telling a new player the whole conspiracy. Lore's suggested mitigations, not yet discussed:

1. **Ambiguity resists spoiling.** "The movement is behind the awakenings" can be spoiled; "why the sleepers have no memory" can't, because the game never answers it. Keep the deepest question unanswered.
2. **Knowing isn't the same as being in it.** Make the reveal the start of content, not the payoff. Per-player engine state gates what a player can act on. NPCs won't confirm anything to a player who hasn't earned it, and a player saying what they "know" is a player claim, never stored as fact (hard rule 6). A spoiled player has knowledge but no standing.
3. **The fiction absorbs it.** Rumours that "someone's waking the old frozen" can exist in-world already. A veteran telling a newbie in character is just one more conspiracy theorist, and the newbie can believe them or not.
4. **Light social norms.** An OOC spoiler etiquette note in help files; maybe a spoiler channel. A norm, not a rule.
5. **Details differ per player.** The broad truth can be spoiled, but each player's own file, revival site and loose ends are theirs to uncover. Optional.

Lore leans towards 1–3, with 4 as light support.

### Options set aside this session

- Why sleepers were frozen: Bloom evacuees with nowhere to go (largely kept, now as the "old frozen"); pre-Bloom long-haul, medical or penal sleepers (kept, as ordinary cryosleep generally); leave it a convention (dropped).
- Why no memory: long storage erodes it (kept as the official story); screened and only blank ones recruited (dropped as the answer, but a theorist could hold it); the movement wipes them (kept only as a theory, never confirmed).
- Movement age: new, with sleepers planned from the start (dropped); a formal split into two wings over the sleeper programme (not chosen; the idealist/zealot spectrum covers it informally).
- Making the goal buildable as a server-wide project with staged milestones (dropped; it stays aspirational).

### Still open

- Which three habitats are joinable, and whether any habitat is reworked.
- The movement's name, and the name of the planned settlements ("New Haven" reads as the real-world city).
- What the zealots actually did: the backfires and the unethical acts the Constellation remembers. Possible surface content: their abandoned sites.
- How each habitat stands towards the movement. Lore's earlier sketch: Anchorage torn between its succession claim and its century of refusing the surface; Hermitage appalled; Freehold would build; Meridian would finance it and want a cut; Lantern wants the biology; Tailings already lives off the surface.
- Where the settlements would go: on or near Haven's ruins, or elsewhere on Prospect.
- When and how a player first meets the movement, and how they come to work with it (or against it).
- Whether a player can oppose the movement, and what home-habitat membership means when the habitats disagree about it.

## Session 1, continued (2026-10-19)

### Owner's leanings

**Temporary memory loss is normal after short sleeps.** It's expected to clear within a couple of weeks, so nobody thinks twice about a freshly woken player having no memory. This settles the base-rate question above.

Lore's note: this gives a natural early beat. The first sign anything is wrong is that the player's memory *doesn't* come back after a couple of weeks. A follow-up clinic visit that ends in polite reassurance does the work, and needs no authored reveal.

### Options offered: names (not chosen)

Names follow the translation convention (§2): meaningful names in plain English.

What the movement calls itself:

1. **Landfall** ("Landfallers"). The Nine's landfall, done again and done right. Hopeful, historical.
2. **The Return.** Nostalgic, with some of the old resentment. Fits the fringe origins.
3. **The Second Nine.** States the plan outright: several settlements, not one Haven. Risk: ties the plan to the number nine.
4. **Groundward.** A direction, not a place. Quieter; suits the idealists.

What the Constellation calls it: **Haveners**, a label from the fringe days, when they did want Haven back. The movement has long since rejected the single city, but the name stuck, and members hate it.

The zealots:

1. **The Sowers.** Their own name: they plant people on the surface. After the disasters, it turns bitter.
2. **Groundbreakers.** Their own name; outsiders use it with a sneer.
3. **Named for their worst incident**, the way "the Bloom" names a disaster. Most realistic; needs the incident named first.

Lore's lean: Landfall / Haveners / the Sowers, with option 3 likely to take over in public speech.

### Options offered: what the zealots did (not chosen)

Suggested shape: one signature disaster everyone knows by name, plus a pattern of smaller acts.

1. **An unsanctioned colony that failed** (backfired). Families, children included, settled at a surface site without permission, to prove it could be done. It failed. The survivors called for help, and habitats refused them dock (§11.6: each habitat decides what it admits from the surface). That leaves guilt on everyone, not only the zealots. Leaves an explorable ruin.
2. **A contamination on a station** (backfired, then covered up). Surface organisms brought aboard to prove they were manageable; a smaller station lost or sealed. A small-scale Bloom, the Constellation's deepest fear. Explains Hermitage's and Anchorage's reactions.
3. **Taking people to the surface by force** (unethical from the start). Debtors, Tailings labourers, or people nobody would ask after, shipped down as "settlers." Echoes the engineered peoples' history as property. Waking the old frozen without consent is a softer version of the same thing, and conspiracy theorists would say so.
4. **Sabotage to make the case** (unethical from the start). Damaging a habitat's farms or life support to prove orbital life is fragile. Clearest reason the movement isn't seen as peaceful; least distinctive.
5. **Reactivating Haven's machinery** (backfired). Restarting environmental or security systems in the ruins to prepare a site, killing salvage crews. Ruins content, and a hazard the movement made itself.

Lore's lean: 1 as the signature disaster, 3 among the smaller acts. Strong alternative: 2, if fear rather than guilt should lead.

### Still open from this part

- Is the Constellation's wariness pure fear and contempt, or mixed with guilt about its own part (such as the refused rescue)?
- Does the movement today disown the zealots, quietly benefit from them, or is it still partly run by them?
- Names for the signature incident and its site.
