Initially, the Three Commissions were justified as life-saving
technological achievements.

Their success sharply reduced the need for ancestral populations to
perform certain hazardous occupations. As entire industries
reorganized around engineered labor, their creators became
economically dependent on the new peoples.

Over generations, this dependence contributed to a profoundly
exploitative social order.

The ancestral civilizations increasingly regarded engineered
populations as resources, inheritable assets, or livestock rather
than independent persons.

By the departure of the Nine, engineered peoples were still legally
treated as property under the prevailing institutions of the home
system.

They could be subjected to restrictions on occupation, movement,
autonomy, property ownership, and reproductive freedom. The exact
legal arrangements differed across jurisdictions.

This treatment was not universally accepted.

Emancipation movements and political reform factions had already
emerged in the home system. Some ancestral and hybrid citizens
advocated legal equality, while engineered peoples organized
resistance and sought recognition of their personhood.

The hybrid-descended people occupied an intermediate social position.
Though generally recognized as persons, they experienced some
prejudice from the three ancestral peoples because of their mixed
ancestry.

Prejudice varied among individuals and societies. The hierarchy was
social, political, and legal, not evidence of any genuine difference
in moral worth.
